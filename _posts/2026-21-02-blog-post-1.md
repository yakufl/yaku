# The risks of big dreams

One of the most prominent thinkers on complex systems, the North American Doyne Farmer, has recently written about how a digital replica of the world economy can serve to plan the paths towards a green future. The ambitious model could be completed with a cost of no more than $100 million and would be a critical tool to guide policy in a world of climate crisis. The idea sounds both straight and powerful. But after reading The Guardian’s article, I kind of wish that project never takes off. 

One argument in favor of this project is that economic models have repeatedly failed to predict financial and economic crises. That is true, but that also ignores the contributions of different schools of thoughts that correctly identified the critical points that were leading the economy towards a recession in 2008. One example: post-Keynesians and its stock-flow consistent model initially developed by Wynne Godley at the Levy Institute.

Big dreams like creating a digital twin of the whole economy tend to obliterate power dynamics. For Farmer the economy can be represented by every firm and machine learning techniques can be used to model decision-making. No national jurisdiction, no global south and global north. The idea is that economics is better represented as an analogy of physics rather than math, as in the current paradigm. It is frankly curious how the word “colonization” is employed to describe that economics has been colonized by math, but there is no such thing as colonialist approach in economics that erases the history of the world economy. The economy cannot be disentangled from the social world and certainly the social world can hardly be assessed through physic lenses.

Since any model presupposes a hypothesis of how the world works, then a digital twin is forcibly subject to design decisions that would reflect the inner model of its creators. As any LLM reproduces certain assumptions and biases, we cannot expect something different from a digital twin. At the end of the day, a model with a 1:1 scale is no longer a model since there is no such thing as a one-size-fits-all in computational simulation. The beauty of modelling is that is a simplified representation of a specific issue. Even more, you can create thousands of different digital twins and, dependent on their inner functioning and parameters, they will spit different results. There is no reason to believe that one specific twin will be undisputedly better. The size criteria are rarely the ultimate criteria.

A frequent trade-off in computationally intensive simulations is that the capacity of a model to predict does not necessarily reflect its explanatory power. As in machine learning, the use of thousands of millions of data points can produce accurate predictions but it won’t help to effectively explain the why questions. What would be the usefulness of a digital twin that is so detailed but becomes a black box? How would that help to infer policy lessons for a green future?

Rather than a 100-milllion-worth digital twin, we would better need a garden of models, a field where different simulations can flourish. With every model as small as possible to address the questions we have, and as big as needed to be empirically relevant. Just because there is the technology to create a digital twin, it does not mean that the model should exist. 

Finally, we are too much immersed in a policy conception according to which experts will save us. What we allegedly need is technical solutions. We cannot neglect the importance of specialized knowledge, but that cannot justify the exclusion of real people and communities from taking part of how to conceive and actually MODEL the world. Any planning, no matter how ambitious or laudable its goal is, if it relies only on the expertise of a certain group, will reproduce social injustices. Planning should be a collective endeavor, a participatory intense process rather than a computationally assisted simulation.

TLDR: A digital twin of the world economy is a bad idea. There are four risks of this: 
1) It assumes that the social world can be represented by physics, and it ignores power dynamics and history. 
2) A digital twin will reproduce the biases of its creators and since it conceives planning as a technical endeavor it will exclude people from their right to take part in a planning process. 3) A model with a 1:1 scale does not solve the trade-off between predictability and explanatory power. An accurate prediction does not necessarily serve to guide policy; it might only offer a giant black box.
