“Envisioning economic democratic planning at scale: An Agent-based modeling approach to simulate plausible
deliverative processes”
*Working Paper to be presented at the 2026 Conference of the European Society of Ecological Economics (Ghent)*

===

Recent discussion on just transition has emphasized the need for democratic and participatory processes to ensure the inclusion of marginalized groups and to achieve environmental outcomes compatible with planetary boundaries (Durand et al. 2024). A timely transition towards a more sustainable society therefore requires exploring new forms of civil participation in public affairs. In this context, the rise of participatory initiatives in recent years, such as citizens' assemblies and mini-publics, has been suggested as a step in the right direction to better capture societal preferences and advance towards a planned social-ecological transition. However, large-scale rollout of these mechanisms would require a deeper understanding of how such institutions can facilitate decision making and consensus-building among citizens, while ensuring the viability of an alternative economic system. This paper aims to provide a solid theoretical foundation to address the current lack of research on this issue.
Current approaches to democratic planning can be traced back to two significant debates. On the one hand, critical assessments of technocratic approaches to urban planning in the mid-20th century opened the way for more decentralized and participatory planning processes in the context of urban development in the US. Proposals such as transactive planning, advocacy planning pointed to the need to break out the knowledge barrier between planners and affected populations. On the other hand, following the peak of the socialist calculation debate, macro- planning proposals emerged with the explicit goal of offering an alternative to the ex-post, market-based allocation of resources. A rift among these theories-- including cyber socialism (Cockshott and Cottrell 2000), Panecon (Hahnel 2020), and coordinated negotiations (Devine 2002)— concerned instrumental versus substantive rationality: the former being more suitable for well-defined and quantifiable problems, and the latter better adapted to complex decisions where optimization is not feasible. This rift is also present in more recent planning approaches such as associational socialism and tech-inspired utopias. We argue that a dialogical planning approach is more closely aligned with the post-growth perspective.
Faced with the difficulty of implementing the proposed planning approaches in practice, only a few attempts have deployed computational simulations to anticipate potential pitfalls and test specific mechanisms. Most of these efforts have focuses on approaches that prioritize algorithms and technological solutions over deliberative processes. To address this imbalance, we develop a “toy model” (Miyazaki 2023) that combines the structure of opinion-dynamics perspective (Das 2014) with an evolutionary-economics modeling (Dose & Roventini 2025). Integrating these perspectives offers a novel view that lays the groundwork for rigorous hypothesis testing of various arrangements based on the dialogical planning. The toy model portrays a simple economy composed of firms, citizens, consumption goods and capital goods. Investment decisions are made through citizen’s assemblies, and the management of the economic units is conducted through the deliberation of the involved social groups. Different scenarios are compared to the baseline model that features an economy populated by profit-seeking firms, in which resources are allocated through the market.
Although the proposed model is a strong abstraction of a real-work economy, agent-based modelling (ABM) allows for the inclusion of heterogeneity in citizens’ beliefs and preferences, as well as heterogeneity across production sectors. Two questions are explored through the simulations: how do these heterogeneities compromise the survival of the overall economy?, and is there a trade-off between participatory decision-making and achievement of wellbeing? The key implication of this paper is that achieving the trifecta of collective wellbeing (defined as the satisfaction of basic needs), non-exceedance of planetary boundaries, and effective and meaningful deliberation is not automatic because the outcome is highly sensitive to specific institutional and behavioral calibrations. Further research could explore more realistic models by including human cognitive processes or using real-world data to calibrate the ABM's parameters. We argue that our proposal of computationally- informed democratic economic planning provides valuable lessons to guide the broader rollout of participatory processes.
